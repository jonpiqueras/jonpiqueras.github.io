Working Papers

Unemployment Insurance, Inequality of Opportunity, and Labor Market Conditions

Young Labour Economist Prize 2023 by the European Association of Labour Economists
EALE Tour 2024
Social benefits are the fundamental reason why unemployment insurance exists, and are therefore central to understanding whether benefit generosity should vary with economic conditions. Yet, little is known about how the social benefits of unemployment insurance evolve over the business cycle, even though downturns may change both the magnitude of consumption losses upon unemployment and who experiences them. I propose and empirically evaluate a framework to assess the welfare effects of extending benefit duration, accounting for selection into unemployment induced by changing work opportunities. Welfare effects depend on social preferences for redistribution across unemployed types, consumption-smoothing gains, and efficiency costs. Exploiting regional and time variation in Spain, where the unemployment rate tripled, I examine the evolution of these components. A newly collected survey demonstrates stronger support for providing benefits to high-willingness-to-work unemployed, whose share increases in downturns. Consumption data reveal larger drops upon job loss when unemployment is high. Regression-discontinuity estimates using administrative records show efficiency costs fall in recessions. Taken together, results indicate that benefit duration should have been longer at the Great Recession's peak than before. Three quarters of the welfare gain from shifting benefit duration from booms to recessions comes from changes in social benefits, rather than from lower efficiency costs. More broadly, these findings imply that existing literature overlooking changes in social benefits understates the optimal duration cyclicality.

The Asymmetric Effect of Wage Floors: A Natural Experiment with a Rising and Falling Minimum Wage

with Emiliano Huet-Vaughn
Exploiting a unique natural experiment, we study the asymmetric effects of a large increase and equivalent decrease to a binding minimum wage. Earnings in a leading low-wage industry rise when the minimum wage increases, but do not fall when it is lowered. The gains persist five years after policy revocation, providing novel evidence of earnings hysteresis from temporary labor policy. Persistence remains during high inflation, contrary to the view that relative wage gains should erode. Our evidence highlights the broader role of labor policy in shaping workers' pay, extending beyond the limits of enforcement and legal incentives.

Search effort and the minimum wage

I assess the impact of the minimum wage on the search effort of the unemployed. Using machine learning methods, and leveraging the richness of the American Time Use Survey (ATUS) together with the large sample size of the Current Population Survey (CPS), I build measures of search effort and exposure to the minimum wage for unemployed workers. I exploit 49 state-level minimum wage changes in the US over 1999-2019 in a stacked-event study design to examine whether the highly exposed unemployed change their search effort in response to the policy. I find that a 10% increase in the minimum wage leads to a 4.9% increase in search effort. Yet, the individuals increasing effort do not find jobs faster. Interpreting the estimates through the lens of a standard DMP model with search effort, I find that the observed effort increase should have raised employment ceteris paribus. However, market tightness declines in equilibrium so that the return per unit of effort in terms of job finding gets reduced, ultimately leading to an overall null employment effect. Moreover, this setup allows me to investigate the welfare impact of the policy in a transparent way, revealing that the minimum wage increases welfare for exposed individuals.
